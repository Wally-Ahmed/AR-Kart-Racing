PLAN A — the car stays stock, the ESP32 presses the remote's buttons

The idea in one line: the car already has a radio and a driver that work — the factory ones — so stop replacing either of them and replace the thumb instead. The remote board is the transmitter it has always been; the ESP32 just takes over its four buttons. The car never learns that anything changed.

THE WHOLE BUILD — tap the battery-box terminals for a JST plug, boost that to 5.00 V for the ESP32, fit the damping mod on the remote, pull the remote's cells and feed it from the ESP's 3V3, run six wires (V+, V−, K1, K2, K3, K4) up to the ESP header, and hot-glue the remote inside the car with its coil end about an inch from the car board's receiver coil. The car board is never opened, cut, desoldered or probed. Three cars, three identical ESP32-S3-CAM boards, one firmware image.

Press and release — the one primitive the firmware needs

The encoder's button pads are active LOW: each pad idles high through a pull-up inside the encoder chip, and a finger press shorts the pad to ground. The ESP32 mimics that with one GPIO per pad. Press = configure the pin as an output and drive it LOW. Release = reconfigure the pin as a plain input with internal pull-ups and pull-downs disabled — high-Z — so the pad floats back up on its own. The pin is never driven HIGH: there is no reason to, because the pull-up does the releasing, and driving it high would fight a human pressing the physical button while you debug. The ESP32-S3's pins come up as inputs at reset, so every button reads “released” during boot and during flashing.

Combinations are free. Verified on the real car: K3+K1 (forward + left) pressed together steers the servo left and drives, because the encoder transmits real combination codes. So the hold model is simply “the current command is the set of pins currently pressed, and it stays pressed until the command changes”. Firmware only has to reject the nonsense pairs — left+right and forward+back — by letting the most recent command win.

The remote board, the damping mod, and the cross-talk we are living with

The board is marked HS-4023T, silkscreened 40M and dated 2025.03.20. It is a small PCB inside a steering-wheel shell, and once the shell is off it is the only transmitter in the build.

FaceWhat is on it
FRONTfour tactile switches K1 K2 K3 K4; pads labelled V+ · V− · ANT; a tunable coil T1 (a grey square with a slot); and a green axial part between T1 and the ANT pad which is the antenna loading coil, NOT a resistor. Four buttons, no turbo.
BACKan 8-pin SOP encoder marked TX4; an S8050 NPN in SOT-23 (marked “J3Y”) as the RF stage; one more small three-pin part; two SMD resistors marked 103 and 104; and some SMD caps. The loading coil's two solder blobs come through here.
Not there at allThere is no crystal. T1's coil sets the frequency, so this is a free-running LC oscillator keyed on and off by the encoder — which is exactly why it leaks the way it does, and why the damping mod works.

There is no wire between the two antennas — this is the 2026-10-03 discovery

The remote's free-running oscillator leaks so strongly in the near field that, with both whip antennas removed, the car still obeyed the remote at one to two feet. A bare wire from the remote's ANT pad to the car's antenna hole worked — but breaking that wire changed nothing at bench distance, so the wire was never the limiting path. Therefore: the ANT pad is not wired, the car's ANT hole stays untouched and empty, and the planned series-resistor steps (10k / 47k / 100k) and the 47 pF idea are all out of the build. Two things replace them.

(a) The damping mod — one per remote, on all three cars

On the back of the board the antenna loading coil has two solder blobs. Tell them apart with a continuity reading from the ANT pad: the blob that does NOT beep to the ANT pad is the inner, T1-side node — that is the solder point. From that node to V−, fit a 47 Ω resistor in series with a 1 µF electrolytic, stripe toward V−. The cap is there because that node carries DC and the resistor alone would load it.

(b) Coil-to-coil mounting

Each remote board mounts inside its own car with the T1 / loading-coil end within about an inch of the car board's receiver coil, in the same orientation every time, hot-glued so a crash cannot shift it. Margin was proved by holding the remote at two to three times the intended mounting distance — every direction still worked, so an inch has plenty of room in hand.

Band fact, and get this right. The receiver boards vary per car: this fleet has a 2.4 GHz car, a 49 MHz car (HS-409R family) and a 40 MHz car. The 40-car's set is 40 MHz — silkscreen “40M”, confirmed by a positive jam test at exactly 40.000 MHz. Plan A does not care which band a car is on, because each remote makes its own carrier for its own car at an inch of near-field coupling. Do not plan around the cars sharing one channel: they do not.

The accepted limitation — and the bolt-on fix

Even at 47 Ω the leak reach is longer than collision distance, and we are shipping anyway, because the clock wins. In practice: a car that is being driven is effectively immune to a neighbour's remote, because its own signal at one inch swamps anything arriving from several inches away. A car that is parked is transmitting nothing, so if another car's remote passes close it will obey it until that car moves away. That is physics, not a firmware bug, and it is probe ④.

THE FIX, IF IT BITES AT THE DEMO — a grounded foil wrap on the car board: tape or paper over the board, two layers of heavy-duty foil folded shut, a short wire to V−, with only the motor, steering and battery leads exiting. About ten minutes per car, it changes nothing else, and a roll of heavy-duty foil goes in the kit. The remote board can be wrapped the same way as a second layer. The ESP always stays OUTSIDE any foil — it needs its Wi-Fi.

SIDEBAR — this meter has no beeper. Every “continuity” check on this sheet is done on the Ω (resistance) range, reading the number: near 0 Ω (under a few ohms) = the two points are connected; a big number, or 1 / OL alone on the left of the display, = not connected. Always touch the two probe tips together first — that must read near 0, or the meter is on the wrong range or a lead is in the wrong jack. And always measure with the batteries out. This cost an evening once already; it does not need to cost another.

Station 2 — The remote board (per car, ~25 min)

Nothing on the car is cut. Not one wire, not one pad, not one pair. The factory receiver board stays screwed down where it is, still wired to the battery, the drive motor and the steering, and it keeps doing every bit of the driving. The ANT hole stays untouched. Everything in this station happens to the remote board and to the battery box — which is why Plan A is fully reversible: pull two tap wires and the car is a toy again. If you find yourself reaching for side cutters near the car board, stop: you are on the wrong plan.

This station is the whole interesting part of the build. The remote board leaves its steering-wheel shell, loses its cells for good, gains the damping mod, gains six wires, and gets glued inside the car with its coil end an inch from the car board's receiver coil.

No header strips, no snapping. Header pins were dropped. For every connection the male end of a male-to-female Dupont jumper is soldered directly to the board and the female end plugs onto the ESP's header — one solder joint per wire and nothing to snap off a strip. Every soldered jumper gets a dab of hot glue at the board, because the Dupont crimp is the weak point and these cars will take hits. The remote board needs six wires; the car board needs none — its taps are taken at the battery box.

Station 3 — ESP32 hookup (per car, 15 min)

Why these four GPIOs — the map moved on 2026-10-04. The button lines are 39 · 40 · 41 · 42 because those are four physically consecutive pins on the right-hand header, confirmed against the silkscreen on the board in hand. Reading down the header they are LEFT · RIGHT · BACK · FORWARD, so the entire ESP-side control harness is one 4-pin Dupont block instead of four strays — and 3V3 and GND sit next to each other at the top of the left header, which makes the remote's power a second, 2-pin block. Six wires, two blocks. Why 39 and 40 are legal: they are µSD lines, and no firmware on this build ever mounts the µSD — that is a standing rule of the project, so nothing else claims them; and whatever pull-up the board may fit on them is harmless, because the remote idles its own pads high from the same 3V3 rail. 39–42 are also the chip's JTAG pins, but JTAG rides the USB port by default, so here they are plain GPIOs. Strapping pins 0 / 3 / 45 / 46 are still deliberately not used for button lines; GPIO3 is an input only (the IR sensor's SIG line) and is strap-inert at the default eFuse setting. GPIO47 and GPIO21 carried K1 and K2 until this change and are now spares — nothing is wired to them. GPIO numbers are printed on the board silkscreen — verify every one against this table as you go, and remember 35 / 36 / 37 are PSRAM.

Bulk capacitance is not optional. Station 4 — the TV-remote-style 38 kHz IR tag pair (per car, 10 min)

The two TSSP77038 sensors are the irreplaceable parts of this build — two sensors, three cars, zero spares. Build all three cars and let the third run without a sensor: it can tag but cannot be tagged. The good news: these are the Adafruit STEMMA breakout boards, not bare parts — the supply capacitor is already on the board, it carries two indicator LEDs, and it comes with a 3-pin JST-PH pigtail, so there is no soldering on the sensor board at all and nothing to get backwards.

THREE WIRES. ONE PER PIN. NOTHING SHARED, NOTHING LOOSE. GND → ESP GND · V+ → ESP 3V3 · SIG → GPIO3. There is no supply resistor, no filter capacitor and no second line into any pin — if you are looking at an older drawing with two wires meeting at one sensor pin, that drawing described a bare sensor and it is gone. And the board tests itself: it has a POWER LED and a SIGNAL LED, and the signal LED blinks whenever a 38 kHz burst reaches the lens — point any TV remote at it and you have a free IR tester, no meter involved.
Why this sensor is the right one for tag: the TSSP770 family is a fixed-gain light-barrier part with no AGC, so unlike a TV-remote receiver it does not hunt for short data bursts and does not fade out partway through a long tag burst.
THE ONE HONEST RETROFIT. If IR reception goes flaky only when the camera is streaming and the motor is running, splice a single 100 Ω into the V+ wire — it works with the board's own capacitor as a supply filter. That is a 30-second fix with the sensor already mounted, so do not fit it up front: build the three wires, and add it only if the symptom shows up.
GATE 3 A phone's front camera sees the LED glow faintly violet when GPIO14 is pulsed. And the sensor proves itself with no meter at all: hold any TV remote at the lens and watch its SIGNAL LED blink. No blink ⇒ check the POWER LED first (V+ on 3V3, GND to the rail); if power is lit and the signal LED still never blinks, the board is facing the wrong way or the remote is dead. Then confirm the firmware sees the same edges on GPIO3.
